Christianity
General / historic Christian teaching
Christians endured persecution for rejecting idolatry
Historic Christianity teaches that early believers were persecuted largely because they refused to worship Roman gods and the imperial cult, viewing such idolatry as incompatible with faith in the one God. Their stern moral conduct and deliberate separation from idolatrous civic practices made them appear socially aloof and politically disloyal to Roman authorities. Christians who were denounced and refused to recant faced deportation, imprisonment, or death, yet the church understood faithful endurance — even to martyrdom — as a witness to Christ. This posture of reproof toward idol worship while maintaining honorable deportment under suffering is reflected throughout New Testament teaching and the witness of early martyrs.
1 Peter 2:12; Acts of the Apostles (martyrdom of Stephen and James); Theology of Martyrdom

Born-Again / Evangelical
Evangelical Protestant emphasis
Honorable conduct silences pagan slander
Drawing from 1 Peter 2, Evangelical teaching holds that early Christians were persecuted precisely because they refused to participate in Roman idolatry and pagan worship, earning false accusations of atheism and immorality. The Evangelical understanding emphasizes that believers were reproved — spoken against as evildoers — by a society whose idolatrous practices they openly rejected. In response, Christians were called to demonstrate upright deportment (honorable day-to-day conduct) so that even hostile pagans would ultimately witness their good deeds and be drawn to glorify God. The goal was not retaliation but a transformed way of life that served as a living rebuke to slander.
1 Peter 2:11–12 ("Having your conduct honorable among the Gentiles")

Catholicism
Catholic Church teaching
Idolatry rejected; martyrdom honors God
The Catholic Church teaches, through the Catechism, that idolatry is a fundamental perversion of the human religious instinct — placing any creature above the one true God — and that it remains a constant temptation incompatible with genuine communion with God. During the persecutions of the first centuries, early Christians were specifically targeted because they refused to engage in Roman imperial idol worship; many chose martyrdom rather than even simulate such worship. The Catechism presents this martyrial deportment — professing faith without compromise even at the cost of one's life — as the supreme witness a Christian can give, rooted in both fidelity to the First Commandment and imitation of Christ himself.
CCC 2113, 2473; Exodus 20:3 (First Commandment)

Jehovah’s Witnesses
Watch Tower Society teaching
Refuse idolatry; endure persecution faithfully
The Watch Tower Society teaches that early Christians were persecuted precisely because they refused to participate in Greco-Roman idolatry, including offering incense to the emperor — a refusal that drew comparisons to the Hebrew youths who defied Nebuchadnezzar's image. Their exemplary deportment (conduct) was not that of antisocial fanatics but of law-abiding, morally upright people whose very integrity set them apart, making them targets. Faithful endurance under such persecution, rather than martyrdom itself, was what secured standing before Jehovah, and the same standard applies to Witnesses today.
1 Corinthians 10:14 ("Flee from idolatry"); Matthew 10:17–22

Islam
Islamic teaching
Endured idolater persecution; conduct remained steadfast
Islamic teaching holds that in the earliest period of Islam, Muhammad and his followers faced sustained oppression from the polytheistic Quraysh tribe of Mecca, who mocked, tortured, boycotted, and even killed early Muslims because they rejected idol worship and called for devotion to the one God, Allah. The Quran describes this persecution (fitna) as the context behind verses like Quran 2:193 and Surah 9:5, which are understood not as blanket commands against non-Muslims but as responses to specific aggressors who broke truces and waged war. The proper deportment (conduct) of the Prophet under this trial — continuing to warn, invite, and show steadfastness — is held up as the model, and the community was eventually permitted to emigrate to Abyssinia rather than retaliate, affirming that migration and patience were primary responses to persecution.

Seventh-day Adventism
Adventist teaching
Godly deportment reproves sin; idolaters corrupt the church
Seventh-day Adventist teaching, drawn from Ellen White's Great Controversy Chapter 2, holds that the blameless conduct and steadfast faith of early Christians served as a living rebuke to sinners, provoking hatred and persecution precisely because their holy character exposed the wickedness around them. When outright persecution eventually subsided, a new and more dangerous threat arose: idolaters who partially embraced Christianity while rejecting its core truths, introducing compromise and corruption into the church. Those who cherish sin while professing godliness resent being reproved, and will betray faithful believers when given the chance, just as Judas betrayed Christ. The Adventist lesson is that doctrinal faithfulness and upright deportment must never be surrendered for the sake of false unity.

New International Version
Bible, NIV voice — same teaching as Christianity
Faithful witness amid fierce opposition
Early Christians faced intense persecution largely because they refused to bow down to Roman gods or treat the emperor as divine, and that kind of bold, uncompromising stand made them look like troublemakers to the authorities around them. When they were called out, dragged before officials, or driven from their homes, they didn't cave — they held their ground with quiet dignity, trusting that living well under pressure was itself a powerful testimony to the watching world. The church has always understood that kind of faithful endurance, even when it cost people everything, as the clearest possible picture of what it means to follow Jesus all the way.

Buddhism
Buddhist teaching
Non-harm, right conduct, universal compassion
Buddhism does not recognize a category of "idolaters" to be condemned; veneration of the Buddha image is understood as an expression of respect toward awakened qualities, not idol worship. When facing persecution, reproach, or mistreatment, Buddhist teaching counsels patience (khanti) and non-retaliation rooted in ahimsa — the ethical principle of not causing harm to others — rather than judgment or retribution toward persecutors. Proper deportment (sīla) encompasses right speech, right action, and right livelihood as the moral core of the Eightfold Path, training practitioners to respond to hostility with compassion rather than violence. The Dhammapada encapsulates the standard: avoid all evil, cultivate good, and purify the mind.

Geneva Bible
Bible, 1599 Geneva voice — same teaching as Christianity
Covenant Faithfulness Amid Idolatrous Persecution
The elect, bound by covenant to the living God alone, could in no wise bow to the dumb idols of Rome nor render to Caesar that worship due only to the Almighty, and so were marked out as enemies of the commonwealth by those whose eyes darkness had sealed. When brought before magistrates and compelled to recant, the faithful remnant stood as living reproofs against all false religion, their steadfast refusal bearing witness that God's covenant cannot be surrendered for the favor of men. Their outward deportment under chains, exile, and the sword was itself a testimony of grace — that the same Spirit who sealed them in the covenant also sustained them unto death — so that the blood of the martyrs became, as it were, the seal upon the covenant promise made to Abraham's true seed. Let the godly therefore take comfort that persecution is no token of God's abandonment, but rather the appointed furnace by which He purifies His people and confounds the wisdom of idolaters.
1 Peter 2:12; Doctrine of the Covenant of Grace
